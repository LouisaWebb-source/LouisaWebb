Book 1: Abducted into Fairyland

A Simple Rescue Mission

Damian’s office had become the administrative centre of a small war within hours of his return.

Reports covered most of the enormous desk, several more had been stacked on the floor beside Simon’s chair, and Ipos Hastur had somehow accumulated three separate appointment ledgers despite Damian having repeatedly informed him that he had no intention of attending half the engagements listed in any of them. Two years of absence had left an Empire technically functioning and practically held together by delegation, habit and the combined determination of Damian’s children not to let anyone realise how badly things could have gone.

Simon stood beside the desk with a sheaf of notes in one hand, spectacles sitting low on his nose while his long black hair remained tied neatly behind him. He had been briefing Damian for nearly an hour and had not yet reached the end of the first category.

“The Southern garrisons remained loyal, although several commanders began requesting confirmation of Imperial orders approximately six months ago. Cassandra diverted most of those requests through the Council. Azarael maintained contact with the military, Jared handled anything requiring a visible member of the Imperial family, and Elizabeth attended enough Council meetings to prevent anyone from deciding your prolonged seclusion was an invitation to become ambitious.”

Damian signed the document Ipos placed beneath his hand without looking up. “Helen?”

Simon paused. Damian’s pen stopped.

“That pause was unnecessary, Simon.”

“I was deciding how charitable to be.”

“Do not!”

“Helen has been socially active.”

Damian looked up.

Simon pushed his spectacles higher. “Extremely socially active.”

“I see.”

“You do not yet, but you will.”

Ipos quietly removed the signed document and replaced it with another.

Damian stared at both men. “I have been home for less than a day.”

“Yes, Your Majesty,” Ipos said.

“And already I am beginning to miss the dungeon.”

Simon’s mouth twitched. “Franziska did say you complained too much!”

Damian’s expression changed immediately. There was no disguising it. Pride. Warmth. Something almost possessive. Simon noticed, because Simon noticed everything.

“She said no such thing.”

“She implied it.”

“My treasure has considerably more respect for me than that!”

Simon looked at him over the rims of his spectacles, but Damian ignored him and signed another order.

Ipos cleared his throat delicately. “Your Majesty, regarding Princess Franziska, there are already several scheduling difficulties.”

“Of course there are.”

“She appears to have acquired a household.”

“I know.”

“A very large household.”

“I know.”

“Containing refugees, several Dwarven clans, members of numerous races, liberated prisoners and what appears to be an increasing number of senior political figures.”

Damian stopped signing.

Simon smiled.

“I know.”

Ipos looked down at his ledger. “And she intends to conduct another rescue operation today.”

Damian’s eyes lifted. “Yes.”

Simon folded his arms. “That was going to be my next point.”

“I already know about the Facility.”

“You know she intends to go back?”

“Yes.”

“You approved it?”

Damian gave him a long look.

Simon sighed. “Of course you did. She’s already wrapped you around her elegant little finger, hasn’t she?”

“I wouldn’t put it quite like that.”

“No need!”

Before Damian could answer, there was a sharp knock at the office door.

The guard outside spoke through the heavy wood. “Your Imperial Majesty? General Michael has arrived.”

Damian looked at Simon, whose eyebrows rose.

Ipos immediately checked something in one of his ledgers. “He was not scheduled for another twenty-three minutes.”

Damian stared at him.

Ipos calmly closed the book. “Admirably punctual.”

“Send him in,” Damian called.

The doors opened. Michael filled most of the entrance. Even without wings, the Angel General was enormous, well over two metres of disciplined muscle and gleaming white-and-gold armour, every line of his body communicating command before he spoke a word. Pure-white hair fell to his shoulder blades, his deep silver eyes taking in Simon, Ipos and the disordered desk in one quick sweep before settling on Damian.

“Your Imperial Majesty.” Michael bowed.

Damian rose. For the first time since Simon had entered the room, the Emperor’s expression relaxed. “Michael.”

The two men crossed the space between them and clasped forearms.

Michael’s grip tightened. “You look terrible!”

Damian smiled. “Good to see you too!”

“I had heard reports.”

“I am aware.”

“I had begun assuming you were dead.”

“Everyone appears disappointed that I am not.”

Simon cleared his throat.

Damian looked towards him. “Except my children, apparently.”

“Most of us,” Simon corrected.

Michael glanced between them and, for the first time, the severity around his eyes eased.

“You are genuinely well?”

“Well enough.”

Michael studied him for another moment, the habit of an experienced commander assessing damage rather than trusting reassurance, before finally releasing his forearm.

“I would have come sooner.”

“I know.”

“The southern positions …”

“Were more important.”

Michael frowned. “They were.”

“I know, Michael.”

Damian gestured towards one of the chairs. “Sit.”

“I prefer to stand.”

“Of course you do!”

Michael ignored the observation. Damian returned to his own chair and immediately reached for another report. That bothered Michael. Simon saw it. Damian probably saw it too and simply had too much to do to care.

“I assume you did not summon me merely to confirm that you survived?”

“No.” Damian read three lines of the report, signed it and pushed it towards Ipos. “I need two hundred soldiers.”

Michael stared at him. “For what?”

“A rescue operation.”

“How many enemy forces?”

“Unknown.”

“Fortifications?”

“Unknown.”

“Location?”

“Another Facility.”

That changed Michael’s expression. Not dramatically, but enough. He had heard enough about where Damian had been imprisoned to understand the word.

“How many prisoners?”

“We do not know.”

“What intelligence do we have?”

“Someone else will brief you.”

Michael’s silver eyes narrowed. “Who?”

“Princess Franziska.”

A pause.

“Who?”

Simon lowered his notes. Ipos became extremely interested in his ledger.

Damian finally looked up. “Princess Franziska.”

Michael waited, but Damian did not elaborate.

“Which Princess Franziska?”

“My daughter.”

Simon coughed.

Damian’s blue eyes shifted towards him.

“Sired daughter,” Simon amended.

Michael looked between them. “You acquired another daughter while imprisoned?”

Damian smiled. “I had an eventful captivity.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.”

Michael’s expression suggested he was beginning to remember exactly why being Damian’s friend could be exhausting. “How experienced is she?”

Damian reached for the next report. “Experienced enough.”

Simon looked at his father. Damian did not look back.

Michael noticed neither. “What military experience does she have?”

Damian’s mouth curved. “She led the rescue that freed me.”

That stopped him. Michael looked at Damian properly.´“The prisoners escaped under her command?”

“She organised the operation.”

“With whose troops?”

“No troops.”

Michael frowned.

Damian’s pride became impossible to miss now. “She was abducted into the Facility herself. She escaped her cell, found allies among the prisoners, discovered what she could do and then, rather than simply leaving when she had the opportunity, stayed and rescued everyone she could reach.”

Michael’s brows drew together. “How many soldiers did she command?”

“I just told you. None.”

“Experienced officers?”

“None when she began.”

“Military advisers?”

“No.”

Michael was silent. Simon watched him with increasing interest.

Damian continued, almost absently because Ipos had put another stack of documents beneath his hand. “She worked with whoever she could free and whoever was willing to help. Freed warriors, a Wolven warrior. A technomancer. Dwarves. Prisoners from races she had not known existed hours earlier.”

Michael stared at him. “And it succeeded?”

Damian finally stopped working. His blue eyes glowed faintly with pride. “She brought them out.”

Michael’s expression shifted. Not admiration. Not yet. Reassessment, certainly, but the reassessment went in precisely the wrong direction. A resourceful Princess. Courageous, apparently. Perhaps unusually competent. Still a Princess! And now Damian intended to place two hundred of Michael’s finest soldiers under her operational command.

“Two hundred?”

“Yes.”

Michael’s voice remained perfectly controlled. “For a mission led by a woman who already successfully escaped one of these Facilities without an army?”

“Yes.”

Damian signed another page.

“Damian!”

The use of his name made Ipos glance up. Simon remained very still.

Damian did not. “What?”

Michael stared at him. “What exactly do you believe is waiting in the second Facility?”

“I do not know! That is why I’m sending you!”

“And yet you want two hundred of my best soldiers?”

“Yes.”

“Placed under her command?”

“Yes.”

Michael’s silver eyes narrowed further.

Damian looked up at last. “Is there a problem?”

Michael considered his answer. “No.”

“There appeared to be one.”

“I am attempting to understand the scale of the response.”

Damian’s mouth twitched. “A sledgehammer?”

Michael did not answer, which was answer enough. Simon looked down so nobody would see his smile.

Damian leaned back slightly. “You think I am taking a sledgehammer to crack a nut.”

“I would not have phrased it that way.”

“You were thinking it very loudly.”

Michael’s expression became colder. “I am responsible for those soldiers.”

“And Franziska will be responsible for the mission.”

That did it! Michael’s jaw tightened.

Damian saw the reaction and became faintly impatient. He had an Empire to reclaim, conspiracies to identify, Councillors to interrogate, children to brief and two years of accumulated administrative rot sitting in piles across his desk. He did not have time to persuade Michael that Franziska deserved to be taken seriously.

“You asked whether there is more to this,” Damian said. “There is!”

Michael waited.

“She is important to me.” Something in Damian’s voice changed.

Simon heard it immediately. So did Michael.

Damian set the pen down. “Extremely important.”

Michael’s expression softened by a fraction.

“She saved my life.”

Michael was silent.

“She had every opportunity to leave me where I was. She did not.”

Damian’s blue eyes held his. “You remember what that means.”

Michael’s face changed. Old history passed between them.

Damian continued quietly. “I once came for you when I did not have to.”

Michael’s jaw tightened. “I remember!”

“I expect you to do the same for her.”

The room was suddenly very still.

“Keep her safe.”

Michael inclined his head. “I will.”

“No.” Damian’s voice sharpened.

Michael looked at him.

“I know what your ‘I will’ means. It means you will command the mission properly, position your troops correctly and prevent avoidable casualties.”

“That is generally what keeping someone safe requires.”

“Not this time!”

Michael’s eyes narrowed.

Damian leaned forward. “She leads.”

Silence.

“You advise when she asks. You provide information when she requires it. You give her the soldiers I am assigning to her, and you make certain every one of them understands whose mission it is.”

“Damian …”

“She leads.” The words landed with unmistakable Imperial authority.

Michael stopped.

Damian’s gaze hardened. “And you do not interfere merely because you believe you know better.”

That was considerably more difficult.

Michael’s deep voice dropped. “If she makes a tactical mistake?”

“You warn her.”

“If she ignores the warning?”

“Then you determine whether she is endangering lives or merely offending your sense of proper military procedure.”

Simon’s mouth twitched.

Michael caught it. “Something amusing?”

“Not at all, General.”

“You are smiling.”

Simon pushed his spectacles higher. “I was thinking that you do not mix particularly well with aristocratic ladies.”

Michael went very still. Damian slowly turned towards his son.

Simon continued, because apparently self-preservation had abandoned him. “You know this, Father, and you still pushed him on Franziska.”

Damian looked at him for a long moment. Then the irritation left his expression. What replaced it was colder. More serious.

“He is the only one I trust to keep her safe!”

Simon’s amusement vanished. Michael’s expression changed too.

Damian’s hand tightened slowly around the pen. “I will not lose another.”

No one spoke.

The words were quiet, but the grief behind them were not.

Damian looked towards Michael. “She will clash with him.”

Simon glanced at the Angel General.

Michael looked faintly offended.

Damian ignored it. “Almost certainly.”

“Father!”

“But she is not weak.”

There was pride in the words now, but not blindness. “She will survive that.”

Damian’s gaze returned to Michael. “That is important.”

Michael held his stare.

Something old and understanding passed between them again.

“You truly expect us to clash?”

Simon made a quiet sound suspiciously like a laugh.

Damian looked down at his paperwork. “Michael, you regard most young noblewomen as decorative complications.”

“I do not.”

Simon raised an eyebrow.

Michael looked at him. “I do not.”

Damian signed another document. “Franziska has spent approximately two days discovering that powerful men are accustomed to telling her what she should do.”

Michael’s mouth tightened. “And?”

Damian’s smile was very small. “She dislikes it.”

Simon coughed.

Michael stared at both of them.

Damian continued writing. “You will be fine.”

“Your confidence is touching.”

“My confidence is in her.”

Michael’s expression went flat. Simon looked away.

Damian finally sighed and put the pen down. “Michael.”

The General looked at him.

“Whatever else happens between you, bring her home.”

Michael’s irritation disappeared. “I will.”

This time Damian accepted the answer.

He nodded once. “Two hundred soldiers. Your best. Major Svensson. Ten specialists you believe useful. Report to the Map Room.”

“When?”

Ipos answered without looking up from his ledger. “Twenty-eight minutes, General.”

Michael stared at him. “Of course.”

He turned towards the door.

“Michael.”

The General stopped.

Damian’s voice had softened. “Thank you.”

Michael looked back. For a moment the annoyance disappeared entirely, leaving the old friendship beneath it.

“You rescued me once.”

Damian’s expression became still.

“If she matters to you that much, I will make certain she returns.”

“That,” Damian said quietly, “is what I wanted to hear.”

Michael nodded and left. The doors closed behind him.

Simon waited three seconds.

“You did not tell him.”

Damian had already returned to the next report. “No.”

“That she is a baby Succubus.”

“No.”

“That she discovered magic yesterday.”

“No.”

“That she does not understand military ranks.”

Damian’s pen paused. “She will learn.”

Simon stared towards the closed door. “Michael is going to assume you sent him to babysit an overconfident Imperial Princess.”

“Probably.”

“And Franziska is going to realise within approximately thirty seconds that he thinks that.”

“Probably.”

Simon slowly smiled. “You are a terrible planner.”

Damian began signing the next order. “I am an excellent planner.”

“This is going to explode.”

“Michael will keep her alive.”

Simon considered that. Then nodded. “Yes.”

Damian looked up.

Simon’s smile faded into something more thoughtful. “And she will not let him run over her.”

“No.”

Damian’s mouth curved faintly. “She certainly will not.”

Ipos turned a page in his ledger. “Your Majesty, you are now two hours and fifty-three minutes behind.”

Damian closed his eyes. “I was happier in chains.”

Simon smiled. “No, you weren’t.”

Damian opened his eyes and reached for the next report. “No.”

A pause.

“But there was considerably less paperwork.”

An Invitation She Did Not Know She Made

The Map Room had been silent for almost a minute, which, considering who occupied it, was remarkable.

Ten of the most dangerous Commanders in the Demon Army had somehow remained after the others left, scattered around the enormous map table beneath the suspended representation of Fairyland. Rashoon Oyal stood with them, her newly permanent armour gleaming darkly over red skin, one hand resting against the edge of the table. Malrik the Infernal Warden, enormous even among the others, had folded his thick arms over a chest now protected by armour worth more than most noble estates; Sythira Bloodveil stood opposite him, her crimson veil hiding everything below sharp maroon eyes; Azrith Doomspire's mechanical fingers clicked softly against the edge of the table while furnace-orange light pulsed behind his eyes. Kharvouk Soulshatter had gone unnaturally still, one pale hand resting on the closed cover of his grimoire. Trazel's chains shifted whenever he moved, the red crystal replacing one eye catching the light, while four-armed Loryth Venombane had occupied enough table space for three people and was examining one of her gauntlets as though personally offended that she could find nothing wrong with it.

Draeven Hellmaw finally broke the silence by slapping one enormous grey hand against his breastplate. “Draeven’s fits.”

Ten heads turned towards him.

The Half-Giant spread his hands. “Do you know how rare that is?”

Xorath's ember-bright eyes crinkled with amusement, heat shimmering faintly around his Charredborn body. “I think we may have identified the least significant aspect of our current problem.”

“No,” Draeven said solemnly. “Draeven considers this extremely significant.”

Malrik closed his eyes for a moment. “We need to tell the Emperor.”

Trazel leaned back against the table, blackened chains sliding across his new armour. “About Hellmaw finally owning something that fits, or about the fact that the Imperial Heir appears to have propositioned all of us?”

Draeven's booming laugh filled the Map Room. “Draeven likes this interpretation too.”

“You would,” Sythira murmured.

“Draeven has eyes.”

“Some of us even have two,” Trazel added, his crystal one gleaming.

Loryth sighed, two hands settling on her hips while another pair remained on the table. “Could we remain on the actual problem for longer than thirty seconds?”

“No,” Serith Voidthorn said pleasantly. The Commander of His Imperial Majesty’s Saboteurs and Infiltrators Units looked almost offensively beautiful leaning against the table in his new armour, charismatic features composed into the expression of innocent interest that nobody in the room believed for an instant. “But we can discuss it between diversions.”

Rashoon had said almost nothing. Neither had Velkris Nightgloom, although in his case it was becoming increasingly difficult to determine whether he was still part of the conversation. The dark-haired scout had begun beside one of the windows and was now standing near the doors without anyone having seen him cross the room.

Malrik ignored all of them. “We are going to tell the Emperor!”

“Before we do,” Kharvouk said, his deep-violet eyes moving slowly around the table, “we should establish precisely what we are telling him.”

“That his daughter made us priceless artefact armour,” Xorath suggested.

“His heir,” Serith corrected.

“His Sired daughter.” Malrik amended.

“His Succubus heir,” Trazel added.

That stopped several of them.

Trazel smiled. “Yes. That part is rather relevant.”

Sythira exhaled through her nose. “Unfortunately!”

Loryth tapped one long black nail against the table. “A personal gift of extraordinary value, individually crafted for the recipient and freely given by a Succubus of Imperial rank.”

“Eleven recipients,” Serith pointed out.

“Ten Commanders and one specialist,” Rashoon said quietly.

Trazel's remaining natural eye moved to her. “No.”

Rashoon raised an eyebrow. “No?”

“You are not escaping this on a technicality.”

“I am not a Commander.”

“Neither is Hellmaw technically a Demon. Yet here we all are!”

Draeven beamed. “Draeven feels included.”

Rashoon looked thoroughly unimpressed.

“The tradition does not require equivalent rank among recipients,” Serith said. “It requires a gift sufficiently personal and valuable to indicate that the Succubus wishes to open negotiations. Not a bond. Not an obligation. An invitation to discuss the possibility of joining her hareem.”

Azrith looked down at the armour covering his mechanical arm. “Then the requirement has been met rather comprehensively.”

That sobered them again. Franzi had not created eleven copies of the same armour. She had watched them, questioned them and adapted each design to its wearer. Sythira's enhanced stealth and light manipulation, Velkris's protection from sudden sound without depriving him of the sounds he needed, Loryth's unrestricted movement through all four shoulders, protections built around Azrith's mechanical limbs, Draeven's sheer impossible size. She had considered how each of them fought, what might kill them and what might prevent it.

“She built the protections around the wearer,” Azrith said, mechanical fingers clicking once against the map table. “That is considerably more personal than issuing equipment.”

Kharvouk's mouth tightened. “Which makes the traditional interpretation worse.”

“Better,” Trazel corrected.

“Worse.”

“That depends entirely upon one's ambitions.”

Malrik's heavy jaw tightened. “We do not know what she intended.”

“Yes, we do,” Velkris said from beside the doors.

Everyone looked at him. The scout's black eyes moved over the armour around the table. “She intended to keep us alive.”

No one immediately disagreed. Serith's lips curved. “She called all of us Lord Commander during the aftermath of the rescue.”

“Draeven enjoyed that.”

“We noticed.”

Serith glanced towards him before looking back around the table. “Forty-eight hours ago, she did not know that the Demon Empire existed. Yesterday she discovered she was a Succubus. Today she learned what our military ranks are, with limited success. I find it improbable that somewhere between the Facility briefing and inventing permanent artefact armour she also acquired a detailed knowledge of traditional Succubus courtship etiquette.”

Trazel's grin spread slowly. “So we have been propositioned accidentally.”

Loryth rubbed one temple with one hand. “Please stop looking so pleased.”

“I cannot.”

Xorath had been leaning against the wall, arms folded across his broad chest while faint fire moved beneath charred skin. Now he straightened. “Suppose she did know?”

Malrik looked at him. “She didn't!”

“I said suppose. If Princess Franziska understood precisely what those gifts meant and deliberately opened negotiations?”

Silence settled across the Map Room.

Draeven's grin diminished. Sythira's expression disappeared behind her veil and watchful eyes. Azrith suddenly found one particular mountain on the magical map fascinating. Kharvouk looked irritated, which on Soulshatter could indicate anything from mild discomfort to imminent murder. Loryth stopped examining her gauntlet.

Trazel looked around slowly and chuckled. “Interesting.”

“Nothing has been decided,” Malrik warned.

“No,” Trazel agreed. “But no one said no.”

Malrik opened his mouth and then closed it.

Serith's smile became almost angelic. “An excellent observation.”

“Shut up, Voidthorn.”

“I said nothing offensive.”

“You were going to.”

“I usually am.”

Rashoon pushed herself away from the table. “She came for me.”

That ended the amusement.

Rashoon's fingers rested against her breastplate as she looked down at it. “Six months.”

Nobody needed to ask what she meant.

“For six months, nobody came! My unit thought I had deserted. The army thought I had deserted. As far as everyone who had known Rashoon Oyal was concerned, I had chosen to disappear.” Her mouth twisted, but there was no humour in it. “I hadn't. I was chained in that place, used as a battery when they needed magic and as a body when they wanted something else.”

The silence became absolute.

“She had never heard my name. She had no reason to believe I was important, no political reason to find me and no debt to repay. She learned there was another Facility, gathered people who could help and came through that Portal knowing what had happened in the first one.”

Rashoon looked around the table at people who had spent centuries judging rulers, soldiers, allies and enemies. “She came for whoever was there.”

Malrik's arms slowly unfolded.

Rashoon swallowed once. “And when I asked her why she was doing any of it, the answer was essentially that it was the right thing to do.”

No one spoke.

For several long seconds the only movement came from the shifting light of the magical map above them. Then Malrik inclined his head. Azrith followed. Sythira's eyes lowered. Xorath's fire dimmed, Loryth went still, and even Trazel's amusement disappeared. Around the table, one after another, they nodded.

Kharvouk was the one who finally broke the silence. “She saw through my little test with the blood.”

Rashoon looked at him.

Kharvouk's pale fingers tapped once against his grimoire. “Forty-eight hours before that conversation, she did not know magic existed. She certainly knew nothing about the established principles of blood magic, magical stabilisation or binding. I offered a solution that should have sounded authoritative to someone that ignorant.”

“You told her to use three drops of her blood,” Azrith said.

“Yes.”

“And she refused.” Xorath grinned widely.

Kharvouk's expression became deeply annoyed, although whether with Franzi or himself was difficult to tell. “She questioned it. Then she concluded that my solution was wrong.”

Trazel's crystal eye gleamed. “Was it?”

Kharvouk stared at him.

Trazel smiled. “I enjoy hearing you say it.”

“My proposed solution was not the correct one.”

“Again.”

“Die!”

Trazel looked delighted.

Kharvouk ignored him. “She had no theoretical basis upon which to challenge me. None! Yet something in the magic felt wrong to her, so she stopped instead of accepting the judgement of the expert standing in front of her.” His violet eyes narrowed thoughtfully. “Most newly awakened magic users either distrust everything because they are frightened or trust anyone who sounds sufficiently certain. She did neither. She listened, tested what I said against what she could perceive, and when the two conflicted, she trusted the evidence.”

Azrith's furnace-orange gaze sharpened. “That interested you?”

“It irritated me.”

“That was not the question.”

Kharvouk glared at him.

Xorath laughed softly. “It interested him!”

“It suggests unusual magical intuition,” Kharvouk said coldly. “Nothing more.”

“Of course,” Serith murmured.

Kharvouk's eyes promised a future death.

Malrik's deep voice cut across them. “We all saw the briefing from the first Facility.”

That brought them back to the more serious matter. Malrik planted both huge hands on the map table. “She was abducted too. That matters!”

Rashoon nodded.

“She didn't choose to enter the first Facility,” Malrik continued. “She woke there. She could have escaped with the Emperor once she had a Portal. Later she could have fled alone. She had opportunities to make her own survival the end of the matter.”

“But she didn't,” Sythira said.

“No. She stayed.” Malrik's dark gaze moved across them. “Then she sought allies. She learned what she could do. She went back through that place and pulled out everyone she could reach.”

“Regardless of race,” Azrith added.

“Or sex,” Loryth said.

“Or rank,” Sythira murmured.

“Or whether freeing them was politically useful,” Serith finished.

Malrik nodded. “She did not know who most of them were. She could not know what they might give her later, and from what we heard in that briefing, she did not ask.”

Azrith flexed his mechanical hand. “That is the part I find interesting. Not simply that she stayed. She adapted.”

Kharvouk glanced at him.

Azrith continued, “She had almost no understanding of the world around her and no established command structure. She identified what she needed, found people capable of supplying it and adjusted every time the situation changed. The same thing happened today with the armour. She did not know the accepted method, so the accepted method did not constrain her. She asked what the armour needed to accomplish and worked backwards.”

“Which is a polite way of saying she repeatedly attempted things everyone else knew were impossible,” Loryth said.

Azrith's mouth twitched. “Yes.”

Xorath grinned. “That is the part I liked.”

“Obviously,” Sythira said.

Xorath pushed away from the wall, the cracks of ember-light across his skin brightening with the movement. “She was frightened in the Facility.”

Rashoon looked at him sharply.

“I saw it,” he said. “Don’t misunderstand. It wasn’t panic or cowardice. It was fear. There is a difference, and anyone who has led soldiers for more than a week knows it. Courage from someone who does not understand the danger is merely ignorance. She had already seen what those people did. She went into the second Facility knowing enough to be afraid.”

“And still went,” Malrik said.

“Yes.” Xorath's smile faded into something more thoughtful. “I have led fools who loved danger and cowards who loved safety. She appears to be neither. I have known her for several hours, so I will not pretend to know why she makes the choices she does. But I know what I watched.”

Sythira nodded slowly. “That is all any of us know!”

There it was, perhaps the most important qualification of the entire conversation. None of them knew Franziska Roseheart. Not really! They had watched her for hours, not years. But these were not inexperienced young men and women dazzled by beauty and power. Every person around that table had commanded others, assessed strangers under pressure and survived because they could distinguish competence from bravado, courage from stupidity and loyalty from performance.

They were interpreting evidence. And the evidence was interesting.

Loryth flexed all four hands. “She stared at my arms.”

Draeven immediately grinned.

“Do not.”

He wisely closed his mouth.

“She stared,” Loryth continued, “and then she started asking questions. How they move independently. Whether the shoulder joints interfere with one another. What range I require when fighting. She looked at my poisons, my bones, my equipment and my physiology as problems to understand rather than reasons to step away.”

Sythira's maroon eyes gleamed. “You liked being studied.”

“I liked being studied intelligently.”

“Important distinction.”

“Extremely!”

Trazel's chains scraped softly over the edge of the table. “I preferred Michael.”

Serith groaned. “Of course you did.”

“The woman has been here for two days.” Trazel's red crystal eye caught the light as his grin returned. “She does not know the rules, barely knows who outranks whom, discovers the Angel General disapproves of something she intends to do and responds by doing it anyway.”

“He was trying to stop her from endangering herself,” Serith pointed out.

“Yes. And she listened to him.”

That made Serith pause.

Trazel's grin faded a little. “That is the important part. She did not dismiss him because he challenged her. She heard his argument, decided the risk was hers to take and refused to surrender the decision simply because he was louder, older, more experienced and very accustomed to being obeyed.”

Xorath nodded. “That is what I meant!”

“She stood up to him without trying to humiliate him,” Sythira said.

Trazel tilted his head. “She did tell him not to worry his pretty head.”

Draeven's laugh shook the table.

Even Sythira's eyes creased.

Serith rubbed a hand over his mouth. “I had almost managed to forget that.”

“No, you hadn't.”

“No. Her anger at him was magnificent!”

Lucien’s amusement vanished.

That was apparently answer enough.

Valerius leaned forwards again.

“She did?”

“I did not say that.”

“You did not have to.”

Lucien cursed his own face.

“She was friendly.”

“Friendly?”

“Yes.”

“Did she look at Dawnhold the way she looked at you?”

Lucien remembered.

Alistair talking.

Franziska politely enduring him.

Then those extraordinary green eyes sliding past Alistair.

Finding Lucien.

Taking him in slowly enough that there had been absolutely no possibility of misunderstanding what she was doing.

His stomach tightened at the memory.

“No.”

Valerius saw everything.

“Oh.”

“Do not.”

“Oh, this is much better than I expected.”

“Father.”

“Dawnhold approached her first?”

“Yes.”

“Made his grand entrance?”

“Yes.”

“Asked for the dance?”

“Yes.”

“And she chose you?”

Lucien shifted.

His father’s smile became positively wicked.

“The quiet banker.”

“I am not merely a banker.”

“You have spent most of your adult life complaining that women choose the peacock.”

“I do not complain.”

“And the most sought-after woman in the Empire looked past him?”

Lucien’s jaw tightened.

Valerius was now openly enjoying himself.

“She looked past Alistair Dawnhold and chose my son.”

“Please stop saying it like that.”

“Why?”

“Because you sound proud.”

“I am proud.”

“That is deeply unsettling.”

“It should not be. I have always considered you the better man.”

Lucien’s irritation faltered.

Valerius’s expression softened for only a moment.

Then, unfortunately, the amusement returned.

“Did you at least remember how to speak?”

Lucien glared.

“I managed.”

“Remarkable.”

“She liked me.”

The words escaped before he could stop them.

Silence.

Lucien closed his eyes.

Wonderful.

When he opened them, his father’s face was completely unreadable.

That was worse than laughter.

Valerius leaned back very slowly.

“She liked you?”

Lucien stood.

“I have work.”

“Sit down.”

“No.”

“Lucien.”

“Absolutely not.”

Valerius’s mouth twitched.

“Did she say so?”

Lucien walked towards the door.

“Goodbye, Father.”

“Did she ask to see you again?”

Lucien kept walking.

“Lucien?”

His hand reached the door.

“Did you kiss her?”

Lucien stopped.

Behind him, complete silence.

He should keep walking.

He absolutely should.

Instead, his father’s voice came, suddenly much quieter.

“You did?”

Lucien turned his head.

Valerius was staring at him.

Not laughing now.

Lucien smiled faintly.

“Good morning, Father.”

He opened the door.

“Lucien.”

“Yes?”

Valerius hesitated.

For once, the formidable Marquess Vellanor looked almost uncertain.

“What is she like?”

Lucien paused.

That question was different.

He thought about Franziska laughing at Alistair.

Her open appraisal.

The complete absence of coyness when she wanted something.

The intelligence beneath the humour.

The strange combination of Imperial Princess and woman who looked as though she would happily escape her own reception through a kitchen window if given the opportunity.

Lucien smiled.

“Nothing like what your spies report.”

Valerius’s gaze sharpened.

“No?”

“No.”

Lucien considered it.

“They make her sound impressive.”

“She is.”

“They make her sound powerful.”

“She is.”

“Beautiful?”

Lucien’s smile grew.

“Yes.”

Valerius waited.

Lucien opened the door wider.

“But they miss the best parts.”

He left.

Behind him came his father’s irritated voice.

“Lucien!”

For the first time in years, Lucien walked away from his father’s Solar feeling very, very pleased with himself.

Book 7: Gilded by Fairyland

Was That the Lesson?

The four councillors were already seated when Damian entered the Imperial Council Chamber.

High Councillor Bael’Zharon rose first.

Matron Councillor Xalithra Veinwrithe followed, then Vorthag Ironscourge and Eluriah Nyxveil.

“Your Imperial Majesty.”

Damian closed the doors behind him.

“Sit.”

One word.

Quiet.

Polite.

All four obeyed.

Damian crossed the chamber without hurry and took his usual place at the head of the table.

For several moments, he said nothing.

That was worse.

Bael’Zharon finally broke the silence.

“Your Majesty, I understand the selection of the Nocturna guards was successful?”

“It was.”

Damian folded his hands upon the table.

Silence returned.

Eluriah watched him carefully.

Xalithra’s posture had become very still.

Vorthag looked merely impatient.

Bael’Zharon inclined his head. “Then what matter requires the Council?”

Damian looked at him.

“My mate.”

The room changed.

Barely.

But it changed.

Xalithra’s fingers tightened once against the arm of her chair.

Eluriah’s shadows drew closer to her body.

Vorthag’s glowing seams dimmed.

Bael’Zharon remained motionless.

Damian’s voice was perfectly calm.

“I recently learned something interesting about Princess Franziska’s visit to this Council before my birthday.”

Nobody spoke.

“That silence suggests you know precisely what I mean.”

Bael’Zharon inclined his head.

“We informed Your Majesty of the planned Portal because it affected palace security.”

“I am aware.”

“And because Her Imperial Highness had not expressly ordered secrecy.”

“I am aware of that too.”

Damian’s gaze travelled slowly across all four councillors.

“I have read the minutes.”

That landed harder.

Xalithra looked away first.

Damian continued.

“She asked you whether you could keep her birthday present for me a secret?”

“Yes, Your Majesty,” Xalithra answered quietly.

“And you told her that you could?”

“Yes.”

“She therefore believed she had entrusted you with a secret?”

Xalithra hesitated.

“Yes.”

Damian turned towards Bael’Zharon.

“And after she left, you decided that because she had not used sufficiently precise wording, you were under no obligation to respect what every person in that room understood she intended?”

Bael’Zharon’s expression remained controlled.

“Your Majesty, an heir must understand that intention and instruction are not always the same thing.”

Damian’s eyes went cold.

“I know what an heir must understand.”

The temperature in the chamber seemed to drop despite the faint heat radiating from Vorthag.

Bael’Zharon fell silent.

Damian looked at each of them again.

“You thought you were teaching her a lesson.”

Vorthag answered this time.

“Yes.”

His heavy voice carried the conviction of someone who still believed the decision defensible.

“A misplaced word in battle creates an opening. A careless instruction in diplomacy may create a war. She must learn precision.”

Damian nodded slowly.

“Yes.”

Vorthag appeared almost surprised.

Damian leaned back.

“She did learn a lesson.”

Nobody moved.

Damian’s voice became softer.

Icy.

“You taught her not to trust you.”

Xalithra flinched.

Bael’Zharon’s face tightened.

Damian looked at them.

“You think you taught her to choose her words more carefully?”

No answer.

“You think you taught her that an Imperial Princess must speak with greater precision?”

Still nothing.

“You should know that what she actually learned was that when she shares something personal with the four people appointed to advise her, you will exploit a technicality in her wording and betray her confidence.”

“Your Majesty,” Xalithra began.

“No.”

She stopped.

Damian had not raised his voice.

He did not need to.

“You knew what she meant?”

Xalithra swallowed.

“Yes.”

“You knew she believed you had agreed to secrecy?”

“Yes.”

“You knew it mattered to her?”

“Yes.”

“And you told me anyway.”

The Matron lowered her gaze.

Damian turned towards Eluriah.

“You advised her regarding Helen’s placement in the sculpture?”

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

“You helped her?”

“Yes.”

“She trusted your judgement?”

Eluriah was silent for a moment.

“Yes.”

“And then you participated in the decision to betray that trust?”

The Shadowmistress’s expression tightened.

“Yes.”

Damian nodded once.

“Thank you for at least answering plainly.”

His gaze moved to Vorthag.

“You compared her to a recruit leaving an opening.”

Vorthag’s jaw set.

“I did.”

“My mate is not one of your recruits.”

“No, Your Majesty.”

“She is your Empress.”

Silence.

“She is my heir.”

No one moved.

“She is the woman who will one day sit where I sit.”

Damian’s voice dropped further.

“And you decided that the appropriate way to prepare her for that responsibility was to demonstrate that her own Council cannot be trusted with a birthday present.”

Vorthag looked away.

Damian let the silence stretch.

Then he looked at Bael’Zharon.

“And your brother.”

Bael’Zharon’s eyes sharpened.

“The Earl of Night?”

“Yes.”

Damian’s expression changed very slightly.

Not anger.

Something colder.

“Do you know what he did to her?”

Bael’Zharon hesitated.

“He tested her.”

Damian stared at him.

“Tested her?”

“It is a tradition of Nightfall Bastion.”

“I am familiar with the tradition.”

Bael’Zharon went silent.

Damian leaned forward.

“He subjected her to the poetry trial because he thought she needed to prove what she was made of.”

Nobody spoke.

“He thought she was soft.”

Damian’s hands remained perfectly still on the table.

“He thought she was weak.”

A faint crack sounded from one of Vorthag’s metal fingers as his fist tightened.

Damian ignored it.

“Franziska.”

His voice finally carried something beneath the ice.

Disbelief.

“My mate.”

He looked at all four of them.

“The woman who spent three days inside Ebon.”

Xalithra’s face changed.

Damian noticed.

“Yes.”

His smile contained no warmth.

“Apparently that detail did not reach Nightfall Bastion.”

Bael’Zharon inhaled slowly.

“Your Majesty—”

“She sacrificed herself to a sentient Demonic forest for three days.”

Damian’s voice sharpened.

“She endured Ebon when beings far older and stronger than your brother would not survive three minutes.”

The chamber was absolutely still.

“And after that, your brother looked at her and decided she required a test of endurance?”

Bael’Zharon lowered his head.

Damian continued.

“She has been abducted.”

One finger touched the table.

“She has been imprisoned.”

Another.

“She has walked through three Facilities.”

Another.

“She has rebuilt mutilated bodies with her own magic.”

Another.

“She has fought assassins.”

Another.

“She has stood between kingdoms and stopped wars before breakfast.”

His hand flattened against the table.

“And your brother decided she needed a poetry trial to determine whether she possessed sufficient character.”

Eluriah closed her eyes briefly.

Damian’s gaze returned to the four councillors.

“What were you thinking?”

Nobody answered.

This time his voice did rise slightly.

Not much.

It made it worse.

“What were any of you thinking?”

Bael’Zharon finally replied.

“We believed she required preparation for the realities of rulership.”

Damian’s expression became incredulous.

“Preparation?”

The High Councillor held his gaze.

“She must learn precision, Your Majesty. Particularly in matters of diplomacy and command.”

Damian stared at him for a long moment.

Then he laughed once.

There was no humour in it.

“You believe Franziska’s problem is imprecise communication?”

Bael’Zharon hesitated.

Damian leaned forward.

“This is a woman who has already made a genuine friend of the Queen Consort of the Ashen Court.”

No one answered.

“A woman who walked into a foreign royal household and somehow came away with Queen Ashara not merely as an ally, but as a woman who trusts her.”

His gaze moved across the table.

“She has three Angels pledged to her personal service for ten years.”

Xalithra’s expression tightened.

“Three.”

Damian held up one finger.

“Lirael.”

A second.

“Caelion.”

Then a third.

“General Michael.”

His voice became dangerously soft.

“An ancient Angel General gave her ten years of his service willingly.”

Silence.

“And you think her wording is imprecise?”

Bael’Zharon said nothing.

Damian’s eyes hardened.

“She speaks to kings, queens, generals, assassins, soldiers, refugees and creatures older than your bloodline, and somehow they understand her perfectly well.”

He sat back.

“The difficulty appears to arise only when four Demon councillors decide they would prefer not to understand her.”

That landed.

Hard.

Damian continued before any of them could answer.

“She requires advisers.”

The High Councillor went still.

“Not adversaries.”

Silence.

“She requires people willing to tell her when she is wrong.”

Damian’s eyes moved to Xalithra.

“She requires people willing to correct her wording.”

To Vorthag.

“She requires people who will explain the military implications of placing a permanent Portal in the Imperial Palace.”

To Eluriah.

“She requires people who understand secrets.”

Then back to Bael’Zharon.

“What she does not require is four experienced councillors setting traps for a woman who has been an Imperial Princess for a matter of weeks and congratulating themselves when she steps into one.”

Nobody looked at him now.

Damian sat back.

“You called it a lesson.”

His voice was quiet again.

“So tell me?”

He waited until Bael’Zharon finally lifted his eyes.

“What lesson were you trying to teach her?”

Bael’Zharon said nothing.

Damian’s gaze hardened.

“Because the lesson she learned was not precision.”

His next words were almost gentle.

“She learned not to trust you.”

Xalithra visibly winced.

Damian did not spare her.

“Was that the lesson you intended?”

“No, Your Majesty,” she whispered.

“Then you failed.”

Silence.

“You failed her as advisers.”

His gaze moved across all four.

“And you failed me as my Council.”

Vorthag straightened.

“Your Majesty, none of us intended harm.”

Damian’s expression turned cold enough to cut.

“Intent.”

Vorthag stopped.

Damian almost smiled.

“How interesting.”

Nobody missed the irony.

“You were quite insistent that Franziska’s intent did not matter when she asked you to keep my present secret.”

Not one councillor moved.

“You insisted that only the precise words mattered.”

Damian leaned towards them.

“Shall I apply your own lesson now?”

Vorthag’s glowing eyes dropped.

Damian let that sit for several seconds.

Then he exhaled.

“My mate trusted you.”

The anger was gone from his voice now.

That somehow made it more painful.

“She liked you.”

Xalithra looked stricken.

“She respected you.”

Eluriah’s shadows stilled.

“She sought your advice willingly.”

Damian shook his head.

“And you were so concerned with proving that she needed to become more sophisticated that none of you stopped to consider what you were destroying.”

Bael’Zharon finally spoke.

“What would you have had us do, Your Majesty?”

Damian looked at him as though the answer should have been obvious.

“Teach her.”

Bael’Zharon frowned.

Damian’s voice sharpened again.

“When she asks a badly worded question, tell her it is badly worded.”

Silence.

“When she overlooks a security consequence, explain the consequence.”

Vorthag’s shoulders lowered slightly.

“When she misunderstands protocol, correct her.”

Xalithra nodded faintly.

“When she entrusts you with something because she does not yet understand that she should demand an oath from her own advisers…”

Damian paused.

“…do not teach her that she should have.”

That silenced them completely.

Damian rose.

All four councillors immediately stood.

He looked at them one last time.

“She is my mate.”

His blue eyes darkened.

“Your Empress.”

Then colder still.

“And you betrayed her.”

No one attempted to defend themselves now.

“Repair it.”

Bael’Zharon bowed deeply.

“How?”

Damian looked at him.

“For councillors who pride themselves on teaching lessons, I should think discovering that would be educational.”

He turned towards the door.

Behind him, Xalithra spoke softly.

“Your Majesty?”

Damian stopped.

“What if she does not trust us again?”

For the first time since entering the chamber, Damian’s expression softened.

Not towards them.

Towards the thought of Franzi.

“Then you will have learned something too.”

He opened the door.

“Trust is considerably easier to break than to earn.”

Then the Demon Emperor left his Council to consider exactly what they had taught their future Empress.
